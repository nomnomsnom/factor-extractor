The rulebook, explained

Why a Bank Isn’t a Bakery

Every company in the checklist gets scored against a rulebook for its industry. Here is what those rulebooks are judging, and why the same number can be good news in one business and a disaster in another.

1 · The problem

The same number, opposite meanings

Last year DBS, Singapore’s largest bank, had free cash flow of minus S$66 billion. Cash flowed out, not in.

−S$66,000,000,000If a bakery did this, it would be shut by Christmas. For a bank it means they lent more money out than came back in that year — which is the entire job. Nothing is wrong.

That is the trap. A bank, a shopping mall, a chip factory and a coffee chain are all “companies”, but they make money in ways that have almost nothing in common. Judge them all by the same numbers and you will reject good businesses and buy bad ones.

So each company is judged by a rulebook written for its kind of business. There are 25 of them, covering all 200 companies.

2 · What a rulebook is

Three parts, always the same

Part one

What decides it

The single thing that makes or breaks this kind of business. Usually not the thing in the headlines.

Part two

What to measure

The four to seven numbers that actually reveal it, each with a level that counts as healthy.

Part three

What to ignore

The famous numbers that mean nothing here, crossed out so you stop looking at them.

3 · Six businesses

How the rulebooks differ

The same three-part shape, filled in six ways. All 25 rulebooks, with their levels, are in the reference below.

A bank

Takes your deposits and lends them to someone else. It is playing with other people’s money.

What decides it
Whether the loans get paid back. You find out three years late, so a good year proves nothing.
So we look at
How much it spends to earn $100 — DBS spends $39, UOB spends $45. How much of its lending has gone bad. How big its own safety cushion is before your deposit is at risk.
And ignore
Free cash flow, profit margin, debt levels

A landlord

Owns buildings, collects rent, hands most of it to shareholders. In Singapore these are called REITs, and a third of the local list is one.

What decides it
The mortgage. Landlords rarely die of empty buildings — they die when the loan comes due and has to be renewed at a higher interest rate.
So we look at
How much of the property was bought with borrowed money (above 40% is getting tight). Whether new tenants sign for more rent than the old ones paid. Whether rent comfortably covers the interest bill.
And ignore
The P/E ratio — its “profit” includes guesses about what the buildings are worth

A software company

Writes something once and sells it a million times. The millionth copy costs almost nothing to make.

What decides it
Whether growth is slowing. The share price assumes fast growth continues, so the moment it slows the price has nothing holding it up.
So we look at
The growth rate itself. How much of each sale survives as gross profit (above 70% or it is really a services business). How much staff are paid in shares rather than cash. Work already sold but not yet delivered.
And ignore
Book value, physical assets — the company is people and code

A chipmaker

Builds factories costing billions, then sells into a market that booms and crashes every few years.

What decides it
Where you are in the cycle. This is the one that catches beginners: it looks cheapest exactly at the top, because that is when profits peak.
So we look at
Profit margins across five years instead of today’s. Whether unsold chips are piling up in warehouses. How many customers it depends on — often only two or three.
And ignore
Today’s P/E ratio, this year’s growth rate

A shop or restaurant chain

Sells to the public from physical locations, and grows either by selling more per shop or by opening more shops.

What decides it
Whether the shops that already exist are selling more. Opening new branches can hide a sick business for years.
So we look at
Sales at shops open more than a year, split into “more customers” and “same customers paying more” — only the first is healthy. How long stock sits unsold. Whether new shops earn as much as old ones.
And ignore
Total revenue growth, which just counts new openings

An oil or mining company

Digs up something everyone sells at the same price. It has no say over that price at all.

What decides it
Its cost to produce one barrel or one tonne. When the price crashes, only the cheapest producers are still standing.
So we look at
Cost per barrel against the market price. How many years of reserves are left before there is nothing to dig. How much debt it is carrying into the next downturn.
And ignore
P/E ratio, growth forecasts, management’s five-year plan
4 · Reading the card

What the dots mean

Filled green. There is a number, and it meets the rule of thumb for this industry.
Hollow amber. There is a number and it falls short. Not a verdict — a question to go and answer.
Solid grey. There is a number but no sensible pass mark, so you judge it in context.
Dotted outline. Not in the data at all. The row names the document to find it in — these are published, just not in any database.

Each card also shows a score, like 4 of 5 testable measures met. It only counts rows that have both a number and a pass mark — usually two to four of them, not all seven.

5 · Plain words

The jargon, translated

Free cash flow
Cash actually left over after running the business and maintaining it. Profit is an opinion about the year; this is what reached the bank account.
Return on invested capital
What the company earns on the money it puts to work. If it borrows at 5% and earns 8%, it is building wealth. At 3%, it is destroying it while looking busy.
Gross margin
Of every $100 of sales, how much survives after making the thing. Software keeps $75, a supermarket keeps $25.
Debt / EBITDA
Roughly how many years of earnings it would take to clear the debt. Two is comfortable, five is the lender making your decisions.
Gearing
For a landlord: how much of the property portfolio was bought with borrowed money.
Cost-to-income
For a bank: what it spends to earn $100 of income. Lower is better, and the gap between banks is real money.
Non-performing loans
The share of a bank’s lending that is not being repaid.
CET1 ratio
The bank’s own money, held as a cushion. If loans go bad, this absorbs the loss before depositors do.
Rental reversion
When an old lease ends and a new tenant signs, is the new rent higher or lower? Negative means the landlord is losing pricing power.
Inventory days
How long stock sits before it sells. Rising means customers slowed down before the sales figures showed it.
Backlog
Work already sold but not yet delivered. Next year’s revenue, visible today.
Stock-based pay
Staff paid in shares instead of cash. It costs you nothing today and a slice of your ownership tomorrow.

7 · The filter

“Fails no rulebook test”

In the checklist you can now filter to companies where nothing in their rulebook falls short. 46 of the 200 qualify — 26 American, 20 Singaporean.

What that list is not. It is not a buy list, and it is not a quality ranking.

A company can pass every test and still be a terrible purchase, because none of these tests look at the price you are being asked to pay. That is a separate section of the checklist.

The tests are also rules of thumb, not laws, and most companies only have two or three that can be tested at all. One company on the passing list is a tiny satellite-technology firm that clears its two tests and has almost no revenue. Passing means “nothing obvious is broken” — it is a shortlist to research, not a conclusion.
